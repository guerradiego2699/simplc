## How they are classified

There is no single official classification, but in practice PLCs are told apart by three things:

- **Size**: how many inputs and outputs they can handle.
- **Architecture**: whether everything comes in one box or is assembled from modules.
- **Special function**: for example, whether they are certified for safety.

Let's look at the most common types, from smallest to largest.

## Programmable relay

Also called a _smart relay_. It is the smallest step: a compact device with few inputs and outputs, often
with a small display and buttons on the front for programming it directly.

- **Ideal for**: simple tasks such as lighting control, a pump, a gate or ventilation.
- **Limitations**: little memory, few I/O, basic functions and communications.

## Compact PLC

The CPU, power supply and a fixed set of inputs and outputs come in **a single housing**. Many models allow
a few expansion modules to be added on the side.

- **Ideal for**: small and medium machines, budget-limited projects and learning.
- **Advantages**: affordable, easy to install, takes little panel space.
- **Limitations**: expansion and performance have a ceiling.

## Modular PLC

The CPU, power supply and each input, output or communication module are **separate pieces** mounted side
by side, usually on a DIN rail.

- **Ideal for**: complex machines and production lines.
- **Advantages**: you choose exactly the modules you need and can grow later; if a module fails, only that
  one is replaced.
- **Limitations**: more expensive and requires more planning than a compact PLC.

## Rack (chassis) PLC

Modules slide into a **rack** with a backplane that connects them. This is the architecture of large
systems.

- **Ideal for**: entire plants and continuous processes, with hundreds or thousands of I/O points.
- **Typical features**: remote I/O racks spread across the plant, module replacement with the system
  powered (_hot swap_) and **redundancy**: two CPUs running in parallel, where one takes over if the other
  fails.

<Callout type="industry">
  In continuous processes —mining, power generation, water utilities— stopping the plant is very
  costly or even dangerous. That is why **redundant** CPUs, power supplies and networks are common,
  able to keep running even if one component fails.
</Callout>

## Safety PLC

A PLC **certified for functional safety** according to standards such as IEC 61508, IEC 62061 or
ISO 13849. Internally it uses redundant processing, continuous diagnostics and special inputs and outputs
able to detect a broken wire or a welded contact.

- **Used for**: emergency stops, light curtains, safety doors, two-hand controls, burner and boiler
  shutdown systems.
- Many current models run a standard program and a safety program on the same CPU, kept separate and
  protected from each other.

<Callout type="safety">
  A standard PLC does **not** replace a certified safety system. Protecting people must be designed
  with safety equipment (safety relays or safety PLCs) and validated against the applicable
  standards. You will see more in the good practices topic.
</Callout>

## PAC (Programmable Automation Controller)

_PAC_ is a term used for high-end controllers that combine the best of a PLC with capabilities close to a
computer: lots of memory, several tasks in parallel, motion control, heavy data handling and advanced
communications.

In practice, the line between a “high-end PLC” and a “PAC” is blurry and depends a lot on how each
manufacturer names its products.

### What about PC-based control?

There is also the **software PLC** (_soft PLC_): a program that turns an industrial computer into a PLC,
running the same kind of IEC 61131-3 languages. It is used when a lot of computing power or integration
with other systems on the same device is needed.

## Quick comparison

| Type               | Typical size (I/O)    | Scalability       | Typical use                          |
| ------------------ | --------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Programmable relay | Few (tens or fewer)   | Very low          | Simple automation                    |
| Compact PLC        | Tens                  | Low to medium     | Small and medium machines            |
| Modular PLC        | Tens to hundreds      | High              | Complex machines, production lines   |
| Rack PLC           | Hundreds to thousands | Very high         | Plants and continuous processes      |
| Safety PLC         | Depends on design     | Depends on design | Machine and process safety functions |
| PAC                | Hundreds to thousands | Very high         | Complex, data-heavy systems          |

## Which one to choose?

Some questions integrators ask before choosing a PLC:

- How many inputs and outputs do I need today, and how many might I need in the future?
- Are there analog signals, fast pulses or motion control?
- Which other devices must it talk to, and over which protocol?
- Are there safety functions to protect people?
- Which brands have good technical support and spare parts in my country?
- What equipment is already in the plant? Sticking to one brand simplifies maintenance.

<Callout type="tip">
  To learn, a compact PLC —or a simulator like the one on this platform— is the best starting point.
  The concepts you learn are the same ones you will use in a rack system with thousands of signals.
</Callout>

## Summary

- From smallest to largest: **programmable relay → compact → modular → rack**.
- **Safety PLCs** are certified to protect people.
- **PACs** and **soft PLCs** are controllers with computer-like capabilities.
- The choice depends on I/O, communications, safety, local support and expected growth.
